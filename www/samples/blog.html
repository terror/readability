Keeping a sourdough starter alive on a schedule

Written by Theo Lindqvist January 8, 2026

For the first two years I kept a starter, I treated it like a houseplant I was slightly afraid of. I fed it when I remembered, apologized to it when I didn't, and baked bread that ranged from excellent to structurally questionable. Eventually I realized the problem wasn't the starter. It was me.

This post is the routine I settled on after a lot of trial and error. It isn't the only way to do it, but it fits around a normal working week, and my starter has been happily bubbling on it for almost a year.

Feed it at the same time every day

Yeast and bacteria don't care about the clock, but you do. Picking a fixed time, mine is right after breakfast, turns feeding into a habit instead of a decision. It takes about three minutes:

  1. Discard all but about 25 grams of starter.
  2. Add 50 grams of flour and 50 grams of lukewarm water.
  3. Stir until no dry flour remains, then scrape down the sides.
  4. Put a loose lid on the jar and leave it on the counter.

That ratio, one part starter to two parts flour and two parts water, gives me a starter that peaks around eight hours later in a reasonably warm kitchen. If your kitchen is cold, use warmer water or a slightly smaller amount of starter.

Use the fridge when life gets busy

When I travel, the jar goes in the fridge right after a feeding. A cold starter can go a week or more without attention. When I get back, it usually needs two or three feedings at room temperature before it is lively enough to bake with.

day 1: feed, rest 2 hours, refrigerate
day 8: remove, feed twice 12 hours apart
day 9: bake

Watch the starter, not the recipe

The most useful thing I learned is to stop following times and start following signs. A ready starter has roughly doubled, has a domed or just-collapsing top and smells pleasantly sour rather than sharp. If it isn't there yet, give it another hour. Bread recipes that say "use active starter" are trusting you to make that call.

None of this is complicated, but having a routine made the difference between a starter I worried about and one I barely think about. And the bread, for the record, has been consistently good.
